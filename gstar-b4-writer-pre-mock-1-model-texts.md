# G-Star Writer 4, first draft: the model texts as writing, with A2 replacements

A companion to `gstar-b4-writer-pre-mock-1.md`. That report checked the models sentence by sentence:
errors, level, facts and syllabus. It did not judge them as **writing**:
- whether each paragraph does one job and leads into the next;
- whether the logic holds inside the story's own world;
- whether words are repeated;
- whether the text shows the devices its callouts teach;
- whether a student can copy its shape in G.

The owner pointed this out on 14 October 2026: L1's jump from paragraph 1 to 2 and the money; L2's
repeated "extracurricular activities" and its unpaired "on the other hand". This file fills that gap.
Two review agents then checked every critique against the draft pages, and every replacement sentence
by sentence. Their corrections are merged here.

**Target:** the owner confirmed **A2** for Level 4 (14 Oct). Each replacement:
- stays at A2;
- uses SB4 grammar only up to the same lesson, and shows that lesson's grammar;
- keeps the D words that E tests, or names the D change;
- keeps the C/F/H terms (*protagonist*, *supporting/opposing arguments*, *summary*);
- gives each C box one answer.

Bold in a replacement marks what a callout points at. Each replacement supersedes the sample texts in
the main report.

## Lesson 1 — Fantasy story

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Jodie has no powers; "a prison she could not escape from" | A good opening image. But the story never says what Jodie **wants**. The Middle callout asks for the goal, and the reader has to guess it |
| 1 → 2 | — | **No bridge.** It jumps from her feelings straight to `Jodie's mom used to tell her the myth …`. Nothing links being powerless to the apple: no `Jodie wanted …`, no moment of deciding to go |
| 2 | The myth; she saves money, packs and takes a boat | `Jodie saved up enough money` is an everyday detail that does nothing in a fantasy quest. It slows the story at the point where it should start. The myth never says **where** the apple is, so the boat has no destination. `If she ate it` is ambiguous (Jodie? her mom? anyone?) |
| 3 | Journey: whale, storm pirates, island, apple | Two dangers squeezed into one sentence (`barely escaped from a giant whale and pirates …`). These dangers are what should prove her brave, yet they get the least space. `It had a strange taste that she could not describe` is filler |
| 3 → 4 | — | `Then, she dreamt of an old lady`. She has not fallen asleep, so the dream comes from nowhere |
| 4–5 | The price | `"You wish for your own power."` is stilted. *power* and *powers* switch back and forth |
| 6 | She refuses | Told, not shown (`She realized that she didn't want to sacrifice others' happiness`). `with a heavy heart` is an idiom above level |
| 7 | She wakes; the moral | **The theme does not follow from the events.** ¶1 says powerlessness is the prison. The end says `The true prison was her own thoughts all along`, but no line shows her thoughts holding her back. What the story does show (she crossed the sea and escaped a whale and pirates **without powers**) is never used. Also: `She might not have powers now, but she learned …` (*now* inside a past story); `ambitious` contradicts her refusal. The Ending callout promises a change in "the protagonist **and the world**", but the world does not change |

### A2 replacement

> **The Golden Apple**
>
> Jodie lived in a world where everyone had superpowers. Her friends could fly, breathe fire, or
> control the wind. Jodie was the only person in her town without a power. **Being powerless was a
> prison.**
>
> **Jodie wanted a power more than anything.** One night, she remembered the myth her mom used to tell
> her. On an island far across the sea, there was a tree with one golden apple. Anyone who ate the
> apple would get one wish. The next morning, Jodie packed some food, borrowed her dad's old boat, and
> set off to find the island. She **refused to give up** until she found it.
>
> The trip was long and dangerous. A giant whale crashed into her boat, but Jodie held on. Then
> pirates who could make storms chased her, but Jodie sailed straight through the wind and rain. After
> many days, she reached the island. An old tree with a golden apple stood on a hill. **That apple was
> the key to her prison.** She picked it and took a bite.
>
> At once, Jodie felt very sleepy. She fell asleep and had a dream. An old woman with a soft voice
> stood in front of her. "Do you want a power of your own?" she asked.
>
> Jodie nodded.
>
> "There is a price," the old woman said. "If you get a power, everyone else will lose theirs."
>
> Jodie **stopped to think**. She imagined her friends without their powers, sad and afraid. She did
> not want to sacrifice their happiness for her own. She took a deep breath and said, "No. Then I
> don't want a power."
>
> When Jodie woke up, she was somehow back in front of her house. The apple and the old woman were
> gone. Jodie still had no power, but she was not the same. She had crossed the sea, escaped a whale,
> and sailed through a pirate storm, all without a power. **Her courage was her real superpower.**

About 310 words.
- **SB4 L1 grammar:** `refused to give up`, `stopped to think` (verb + to-infinitive).
- **D words** in the text: *myth*, *sacrifice*, *protagonist* (callouts).
- **Metaphors:** three. Prison is pre-filled in C; key and courage are for students to find. The last one is the theme, and the events prove it.
- **C dependency:** she gets the apple but chooses not to use the wish, so C must not ask "succeed or fail" (W4D-08). Use the redesign's `The choice` row. C's pre-filled metaphor line changes to `Being powerless was a prison.`

**Callouts:**
- **Opening** (¶1): `Introduce the protagonist and the world he or she lives in.`
- **Middle** (¶2–7): `Say what the protagonist wants. Show how he or she tries to get it, what problem he or she faces, and what he or she decides.`
- **Ending** (¶8): `Show how the protagonist has changed.`
- **Metaphor** (on ¶1's last sentence): `A metaphor says that one thing is another thing, without "like" or "as". Ex. Being powerless was a prison.`

## Lesson 2 — Argumentative text

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| all | — | **`extracurricular activity/activities` appears nine times.** It is a five-syllable word that fills a line of every paragraph. Name the topic in short words (*after-school clubs*), gloss the long word once, then say *clubs* |
| 1 | Defines the term; says students may choose | `Students are free to choose …` and `Usually, students don't have to join … if they don't want to` say the same thing twice. **No question or position is set up**, so ¶2 starts cold. The writer's view appears only in the last line |
| 2 | Three benefits in one run | `so students can grow and become more confident in themselves` chains three ideas. There is no example, so students have nothing concrete to copy. `teach students how to manage their time` returns as the conclusion's condition, so the argument goes in a circle |
| 3 | Drawbacks | **Opens with `On the other hand` with no `On the one hand` before it.** That is grammatical, but the callout teaches the pair and the model shows only half. The paragraph gives one drawback (grades) twice (`can't study well` / `putting aside their schoolwork and studies`) |
| 4 | Closing | It brings in a **new** reason (`learn new things and grow`) and does not sum up both sides. `All things considered` is B1 |
| callout | — | It lists `on the contrary`, which corrects a false statement and does not contrast two sides. The callout teaches a misuse |

### A2 replacement

> **Should Students Join After-School Clubs?**
>
> After-school clubs (also called extracurricular activities) are activities that students do after
> class, such as an art club, a soccer team, or a science club. Many schools have them, and students
> can choose to join or not. So, is joining a club a good idea?
>
> **On the one hand**, clubs have many **benefits**. Students can try new things and find out what they
> are good at. They can also make friends with people who like the same things. For example, a
> student on a soccer team can make friends who love soccer as much as she does. Some students enjoy
> their clubs **so much that** they feel happier and more confident at school.
>
> **On the other hand**, some people **oppose** clubs because they take a lot of time. Some clubs meet
> **so often that** students feel too tired to study, and their grades may go down. For example, a
> student on a soccer team may practice every day and get home late. Clubs can also take time away
> from family and rest.
>
> Clubs have both benefits and **drawbacks**. **However**, I think the benefits are more important.
> Students should choose only one or two clubs so that they still have time to study and rest.

About 195 words.
- **SB4 L2 grammar:** `so much that` and `so often that`.
- **D words:** *benefit*, *drawback* and *oppose* are all in B. *Oppose* also explains the organizer's label "Opposing".
- **The closing** sums up, then gives a position with a fair condition.
- **Two paragraphs, one example:** the same soccer player appears on both sides, so students see how one example can serve both.

**Callouts:**
- **Title:** `Use a question as the title.`
- **Introduction:** `Explain the topic and ask the question.`
- **Body:** `Give supporting arguments in one paragraph and opposing arguments in the next.`
- **Contrast words:** `Use contrast words: on the one hand, on the other hand, however.`
- **Closing:** `Sum up both sides. Then say which side you agree with and why.`

## Lesson 3 — What I'll be doing in ten years

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Becoming a doctor | Mostly **facts about medical training** (`6-8 years`, `helping other doctors and watching them work`), not a picture of the writer's life. `I have to study for 6-8 years` is a present tense inside a future text. `any mistake can cost a person's life` is heavy for a hopeful opening |
| 2 | Daily study | Good. The link sentence (`studying won't be the only thing …`) works |
| 3 | A club | `I'll be joining an extracurricular activity`, `I'll probably be joining a book club`: **the future continuous used for a one-off event**, a misuse the model would teach. Half the paragraph lists what the writer **won't** do |
| 3 → 4 | `I want to be able to balance rest and studying.` → `Speaking of rest, I probably won't be spending my weekends at home.` | **A non-sequitur.** Not being home is not rest. The link has the right form (last sentence → first sentence) but makes no sense |
| 4 | Family/home | Body 3 is labelled `Family, Home, or Friends`, but the paragraph is about **being away**: a dormitory, a four-hour drive, home only for holidays. `since my school on the other side` is missing its verb |
| 5 | Ending | `Above is how I imagine my life will be like` has two errors. The question ending is good |
| title | `Studying Seriously at School to Succeed` | `at School` breaks the alliteration |

### A2 replacement

> **Studying Seriously to Succeed**
>
> Ten years from now, I'll be twenty-one, and **I'll be studying** in the medical program at Green Hill
> University. It will be hard work. Doctors need to know a lot about the human body, so they study for
> many years. But I have wanted to help sick people since I was little, and I'm absolutely sure it
> will be worth it.
>
> On a normal day, I'll be going to classes from morning until afternoon. Medical students **are
> supposed to** read a lot before every class, so in the evening I'll be reading in the library.
> Before big exams, I'll probably be studying with my classmates. **However, studying won't be the
> only thing I'll be doing.**
>
> **I'll also be playing guitar in the university band.** We'll practice twice a week and play at
> university events. Music will help me relax after a long day. **Still, my favorite way to relax will
> be spending time with my family.**
>
> **On weekends, I'll be taking the train home to visit them.** My little brother will be in high
> school by then, so I'll help him with his homework. On Sunday nights, we'll all eat dinner together
> before I go back to my dormitory.
>
> This is how I imagine my life in ten years. What about you? What will you be doing?

About 210 words.
- **SB4 L3 grammar:** the future continuous for ongoing activities only, mixed with *will*; `are supposed to`.
- **Links:** both pairs make sense. Study turns to the band; then relaxing turns to family.
- **Body 3** is a family scene.
- **D words:** *program* and *absolutely* are in the text. Drop *admission*, *hardship* and *registered* (main report, L3).
- **C:** its Introduction bullets and pre-filled link sentence change to match.

**Callouts:**
- **Title:** `Use alliteration in your title. Alliteration is when words that are close together begin with the same sound.`
- **Introduction:** `Say what you will be doing and what it will be like.`
- **Body:** `Connect your paragraphs: the last sentence of one leads into the first sentence of the next.`
- **Ending:** `End with a question to the reader.`

## Lesson 4 — Science fiction: an imagined future

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| frame | — | `In my imagined future, humans would still live on Earth` labels the text instead of framing it. The G prompt uses *would*, so the model should show why: SB4 L4's `If I could …, I would …` |
| 1 | Introduction | No time (when is this?). The Moon holiday is mentioned and never comes back. `all kinds of awesome tools and equipment` is vague and informal |
| 2 | Invention 1 | Compares science fiction to **fantasy** (`Just like how witches and wizards … could teleport`), the genre the yellow note says it is not. An `elevator` that crosses the world is a confusing image |
| 2 → 3 | `First,` … `Scientists would invent …` | No `Second,`. ¶2 has *humans would be able*, ¶3 *scientists would invent* (a future inside the future) |
| 4 | Free time → the goal | It starts with free time and switches to `Historians and wildlife researchers … extinct animals`, so the goal is hidden mid-paragraph. Historians do not bring animals back |
| 5 | Conclusion | Generic (`many differences`, `more convenient`, `for the sake of humanity`), with an error: `since we have more time` → `would have`. It does not compare with today, though the callout asks for a comparison |

### A2 replacement

> **Life in the Year 2100**
>
> **If I could travel to the year 2100, I would see a very different world.** People would still live
> on Earth, but life would be much easier.
>
> The biggest change would be how people travel. There would be travel pods in every city. You would
> step inside a pod, say where you wanted to go, and arrive there **instantly**. The pods would not
> make any **pollution**, and they could even take families to the Moon for a holiday. Nobody would sit
> on a plane for twelve hours anymore.
>
> Another change would be in people's kitchens. Every home would have a food machine. You would tell
> it what you wanted to eat, and a hot, healthy meal would be ready in two minutes. Cooking would
> become a hobby, not a chore.
>
> With so much free time, people would work toward big goals. **Scientists would try to bring back
> animals that no longer live on Earth, like the dodo.** It might take them a hundred years, but they
> would not give up, **for the sake of** the animals.
>
> **Life in 2100 would be very different from life today.** Today, we spend hours traveling and
> cooking. In 2100, we would have more time for our families and our dreams. However, people might
> also walk less and become lazy. Still, I believe the future would be better.

About 215 words.
- **SB4 L4 grammar:** a second conditional frame, then *would* throughout.
- **Structure:** two inventions fill C's two slots; the goal has its own paragraph.
- **Conclusion:** compares 2100 with today and gives a good side, a bad side and an opinion.
- **D words:** *instantly*, *pollution* and *for the sake of* are in the text. Replace *source* and *self-service* (main report, L4).
- **Picture:** the dodo needs one.

**Callouts:**
- **Introduction:** `Start with "If I could …, I would …" and say when your future is.`
- **Body:** `Describe two inventions and how they would change daily life.`
- **Goal:** `Write a goal that people in the future would work toward.`
- **Conclusion:** `Compare the future with today. Give a good side, a bad side, and your opinion.`

## Lesson 5 — Announcement

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| frame | — | **It is a press statement, not an announcement.** It defends a company against rumours and apologizes. A Level 4 student will announce a school fair or a train delay (F's ideas) |
| title → 1 | Title: a cancellation. ¶1: the purpose is `to clear up some misinformation` | The callout `Write the purpose` points at a purpose that is not the news in the title |
| 2 | The cancellation | Good content in a heavy passive (`preparations were being carried out when we received news`) |
| 3 | `Secondly, PlayTV did not hide the news … to keep people from refunding their tickets.` | **It answers an accusation the reader has never heard**, and the refund details are mixed into a denial |
| 4 | The rumour of an argument | An unnumbered third point. `Undeniably, this is false` is the only emphasis word, and it is unidiomatic |
| 5 | Closing | Apology, thanks and `Please continue supporting us`: a corporate formula. **There is no call to action**, though the callout offers one |

### A2 replacement

> **Announcement: Sports Day Moved to May 22**
>
> Dear students and families,
>
> Sports Day will not **take place** on May 15 as planned. It will now be on May 22, one week later.
>
> First, the running track **is being repaired** this week, so students **can't** use it. Workers are
> fixing holes in the track, and it will **definitely** be ready by May 22.
>
> Second, you do not need to **sign up** again. If you signed up for a race, you will still **be able
> to** run in it.
>
> Third, the **schedule** for the day has not changed. Sports Day will start at 9:00 a.m. and end at
> 2:00 p.m. The **location** is the same: the school field. Families are **certainly** welcome to come
> and watch.
>
> We are sorry for the change, and thank you for understanding. **Reminder:** please bring a hat and a
> water bottle, and come and cheer for your classmates!
>
> Lakeview School Office

About 135 words.
- **SB4 L5 grammar:** the passive continuous (`is being repaired`), `can't` and `be able to`.
- **Structure:** an introduction, three numbered points, and a closing with an apology, a thank-you and a call to action. That matches G's five markers and H.
- **Emphasis words:** two, each about something the writer can be sure of.
- **D words:** *take place, sign up, schedule, location, reminder* are all in the text.
- **C/F/H:** C's pre-filled Body 1 changes to `• The track is being repaired this week. • It will be ready by May 22.` Goal 2 and the H row change from "transition words for emphasis" to "words that show you are sure". H row 2 (`begins with the purpose`) becomes `begins with the most important news`.

**Callouts:**
- **Title:** `Begin the title with "Announcement:" and say what is happening.`
- **Introduction:** `Say the most important news first.`
- **Body:** `Give one point in each paragraph.`
- **Emphasis words:** `Use words that show you are sure, such as definitely and certainly.`
- **Closing:** `End with a thank-you or an apology, and tell readers what to do.`

## Lesson 6 — Opinion essay

### The draft as writing

| ¶ | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A question, two big statistics (5 billion, 4 billion), "two main causes" | **An explanation, not an opinion.** `In my opinion, there are two main causes` gives causes, not a view, so the essay never says what the writer *believes* |
| 2 | Cause 1: hunger | Two more big figures in one sentence, then a sudden policy demand (`Government … should make sure`). The opinion (`should be addressed as soon as possible`) is about world hunger, not nutrients |
| 3 | Cause 2: allergies | **Weak logic:** food allergies (33 million in the US) cannot explain 5 billion people short of vitamins. Then a medical aside on lactose that is not an allergy (W4D-33) |
| 4 | Conclusion | Moves to new ideas (`food security`, `raise awareness on alternative food choices`) that the body did not argue |
| callout | — | It lists seven opinion phrases; B models three |

### A2 replacement

> **Why Children Should Go to Bed Earlier**
>
> Everyone needs sleep, **don't they**? But many children do not get enough. **According to** doctors,
> children ages 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep every night. I did a **survey** of the 25 students
> in my class, and only 8 of them sleep nine hours or more. That is only 32 **percent**. **In my
> opinion, children should go to bed earlier**, for two reasons.
>
> First, sleep helps us learn. When we sleep, our brains store what we learned during the day. **From
> my point of view**, a student who sleeps well will remember more in class. In my survey, 12 of the
> 17 students who sleep less than nine hours said they often forget things at school.
>
> Second, sleep keeps us **healthy**. According to doctors, children who do not get enough sleep get
> sick more easily. **It seems to me that** tired children are also in a bad mood more often.
>
> In conclusion, **I believe** children should go to bed earlier. If they sleep more, they will learn
> better and feel healthier. Tonight, why not put your phone away an hour before bed and go to sleep
> earlier?

About 180 words.
- **SB4 L6 grammar:** the tag question (`don't they?`).
- **Structure:** one opinion, kept the same in the title, the thesis and the conclusion. Two reasons, each with a fact that supports **that** reason.
- **Where the facts come from:** doctors (CDC and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine: 9–12 hours for ages 6–12) and the writer's own survey. The survey shows students how to collect real data, replacing the draft's "make up your own data" (W4D-36).
- **Opinion phrases:** four, all of them in the callout.
- **D words:** *survey, percent, according to, healthy* are in the text.
- **F:** swap the idea `why naps are important for us`, or students will copy this text. Suggestion: `why children should play outside every day`.

**Callouts:**
- **Introduction:** `Introduce the topic with a question or a fact. Then give your opinion.`
- **Body:** `Give each reason in its own paragraph, with a fact or a number to support it.`
- **Opinion phrases:** `Use phrases that show your opinion: in my opinion, from my point of view, it seems to me that, I believe.`
- **Conclusion:** `Restate your opinion and sum up your reasons.`

## Lesson 7 — Five-part story

### The draft as writing

| Part | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposition | The Scavengers; Iggy taken | Opens with the villains' habits (`would steal robots`), not the hero. The theft (the inciting incident) happens **here**, before the rising action. No time or place. `worth more than a billion credits` makes the bond about money |
| Rising action | Allen; the warehouses; Iggy in pieces | Allen arrives in a sentence about *his* case. The search takes one sentence. The story's main event (Iggy destroyed) gets two words: `They were too late` |
| Climax | Revenge: she blows up warehouses for months | **No decision**, though the callout promises one; a montage of violence. Allen `tried to stop her, but she always succeeded in avoiding him`, and his part of the plot is dropped |
| Falling action | She falls into trash, bleeding; finds a toy | The turn comes from an accident, not a choice. `She cried` is used for weeping and then for shouting |
| Resolution | She yells for help and decides to restart her life | Help from whom? It is never answered. The ending is both sad and open (W4D-57). The theme (stop living for revenge) comes from nowhere, because she never questioned the revenge |

### A2 replacement

> **Helena and Iggy**
>
> In the year 2150, Neo City was full of flying cars and helpful robots. Twelve-year-old Helena lived
> there with Iggy, a small robot dog. Iggy was a gift from her grandparents, and he was her best
> friend.
>
> **One rainy evening, Helena left Iggy at home while she went to the store. When she came back, the
> door was open and Iggy was gone.** On the floor, there was a card with one word on it: "Scavengers."
> The Scavengers were a group that stole robots and sold their parts. Helena was very angry. "I'll
> make them pay," she said.
>
> Helena went to the police, and Detective Allen agreed to help her on her **mission**. Together, they
> searched old warehouses all over the city. They found many stolen robots, but no Iggy. Once, a guard
> robot almost caught them. Every night, Helena thought, "**If I had stayed home, this wouldn't have
> happened.**"
>
> At last, they found the Scavengers' hideout. Inside, **Helena saw Iggy lying in pieces** on a table.
> She **heard a man laughing** in the next room. Detective Allen ran after the Scavengers' leader. Then
> Helena saw a message on a computer screen: "Deleting memory chip … 10 seconds." **She could chase the
> leader and make him pay, or she could save Iggy's memories.** Helena ran to the computer and pulled
> out the chip.
>
> The leader **escaped**, but Detective Allen caught two of his men. Helena held the chip tightly. "I
> wanted to make them pay," she said, "but I almost lost Iggy forever." The detective smiled. "You
> made the right choice."
>
> A week later, a robot doctor rebuilt Iggy and put his old chip back in. When Iggy opened his eyes,
> he wagged his tail and jumped into Helena's arms. He remembered everything. Helena smiled. **She knew
> now that rescuing a friend was more important than punishing the Scavengers.**

About 330 words.
- **SB4 L7 grammar:** the third conditional, now with a cause in the story (she left him at home); perception verbs (`saw Iggy lying`, `heard a man laughing`).
- **Parts:**

  | Part | Paragraphs |
  | --- | --- |
  | Exposition | ¶1 |
  | Rising action | ¶2–3. The inciting incident opens ¶2, and the problems grow: a dead end, then the guard robot |
  | Climax | ¶4, a real choice |
  | Falling action | ¶5, the consequence |
  | Resolution | ¶6. Clearly **happy**, so `Type of Ending` has one answer |

- **Helena changes:** at first she wants to "make them pay"; at the end she values rescuing a friend above punishment. There is no gun, no violence and no revenge.
- **D words:** *mission, escape, rescue* (and *detective*) are in the text. Replace *revenge*, *kidnap* and *harsh* (main report, W4D-38).
- **C key for Characters:** Helena and Iggy, plus Detective Allen.

**Callouts:**
- **Exposition:** `Introduce the characters and the setting (when and where).`
- **Rising action:** `Start with the inciting incident: the problem that starts the story. Then show how things get harder.`
- **Climax:** `The most exciting part. The protagonist makes a hard choice.`
- **Falling action:** `Show what happens because of the choice.`
- **Resolution:** `Show how the story ends and how the protagonist has changed.`

## Lesson 8 — Non-chronological report

### The draft as writing

| Part | What it does | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | `Sugar is a sweetener in food and beverages.` | Circular (sugar is a sweetener). A technical word (*sucrose*) arrives in sentence 3. It never says what the report will cover |
| Kinds of Sugar | Lists sugars | `even when they don't taste sweet sometimes` is muddled; the starch point is wrong (W4D-55); the milk sugar is wrong (W4D-54) |
| What Sugar Is For | Glucose = energy | `Glucose, which is another type of sugar` reintroduces a sugar already named. The soda example sits oddly in a positive section |
| Why reduce | Weight, teeth | Two claims, with no number and no advice. The subheading `Why People Should Reduce the Sugar They Eat` is too long to model |
| Did You Know? | Diabetes | **Changes topic** (from a food to a disease), does not address the reader, and is the hardest paragraph in the book |

### A2 replacement

> **Sweet Facts About Sugar**
>
> Sugar makes food and drinks taste sweet. Most of it comes from two plants, sugarcane and sugar
> beets. **This report tells you about the different types of sugar, what sugar does in your body,
> and why we should eat less of it.**
>
> **Types of Sugar**
> There are different **types** of sugar. The white sugar in your kitchen is sucrose. Fruit and honey
> **contain** fructose, and the sugar in milk is lactose. Some foods, like bread, rice, and potatoes, do
> not taste sweet, but they contain starch. Your body changes starch into another type of sugar,
> glucose.
>
> **What Sugar Does**
> Glucose is your body's main **source** of **energy**. It **gives your brain and muscles the power**
> they need to work. When you run, think, or play, your body is using glucose. That is why you feel
> tired and hungry when you have not eaten for a long time.
>
> **Why We Should Reduce Sugar**
